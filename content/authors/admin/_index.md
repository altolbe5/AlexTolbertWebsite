I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Data and Decision Sciences at Emory University, with secondary appointments in Philosophy, Computer Science, and African American Studies. I am a Faculty Fellow at the Emory Center for Ethics and a Faculty Affiliate of the Center for Mind, Brain, and Culture.

I work between causal inference and the philosophy of science, on the assumptions that statistical methods have to make before their conclusions mean anything. Three problems keep recurring, and most of what I write is some version of one of them.

The first is what has to be true of a variable before it can be treated as a cause. Causal models assume you can move one variable and leave the rest where they are. Many of the variables that carry the most weight in the social sciences do not behave that way: they are bundles, and pulling on one part drags the others along. I work on when that failure sinks a causal claim outright and when it can be repaired by describing the system at a coarser grain.

The second is what can be said about a single person rather than a population. Any individual belongs to many reference classes at once, and different classes yield different predictions, with nothing in the statistics to adjudicate between them. The problem is real for the single case and largely dissolves at scale: a forecaster who has to be right about many people over time is held to a far stricter standard than one who has to be right about one. I work on what that standard amounts to, and on what follows when two well-informed forecasters disagree.

The third is how a mistaken belief survives contact with evidence. A lender who overestimates how often a group defaults stops lending to that group, and so never observes the repayments that would have corrected the estimate. The error is stable precisely because acting on it destroys the data that would refute it. These traps have a useful structure: an intervention that is finite and temporary can be enough to break one, and the corrected state holds after the intervention ends.

Alongside this I work in non-ideal political theory, on what changes when institutions are treated as they actually are rather than as they ought to be — which is usually where the interesting constraints live.

I completed my Ph.D. in Philosophy at the University of Pennsylvania alongside a Masters in Statistics from the Wharton School. Before that I took master's degrees in Biochemistry and Philosophy at Virginia Tech and a B.S. in Biology at the University of Mobile.
